6. Conclusions

This study set out to build a computational path from public transcriptomic data to a ranked list of repurposing candidates for acute myocardial infarction, and to test that path hard enough to find out where it breaks. It breaks in three specific places, and in one place it holds.

Where it holds. PDE4B emerges as the highest-priority follow-up candidate in this study. Several lines of evidence point the same way, but they are not independent: it is the only gene selected in all five cross-validation folds (folds that share training samples), it is the only gene that passes a strict disease-association threshold among 28 candidates drawn from the same screen, it carries 28 known drug candidates and the highest ChEMBL potency annotation in the pool, and it is the subject of a published study in which global PDE4B deletion, bone-marrow chimeras and vascular PDE4B experiments all pointed to a protective effect [12]. Database evidence, literature evidence and cross-validation evidence share underlying provenance, so their agreement is supporting rather than confirmatory. Its direction of change in patients (up-regulated) is compatible with the inhibition hypothesis, but direction of change does not by itself establish the therapeutic direction.

Where it breaks. First, the transcriptomic signature does not transport: the nested AUC of 0.961 within CD146+ endothelial cells falls to 0.608 in whole blood, with a confidence interval that includes chance, and the locked threshold yields zero sensitivity. Second, the ligand models showed no anomaly under label permutation and retained some target-specific discriminative information under cross-target hard negatives, yet both benchmarks are saturated (AP 0.97–0.99) and were built from a positive class restricted to sub-30 nM ligands, one to two orders of magnitude more potent than the approved drugs they were applied to. Third, the approved-drug ranking fails its own control test: of two evaluable known PDE4 inhibitors it recovers one (crisaborole, rank 1/1,823) and misses one (ibudilast, rank 1,282/1,823, score 0.008), while placing a chemically related non-PDE4 drug second (tavaborole). This is a partial recovery under a limited control set, not a stable sensitivity estimate. The pre-specified criterion required two independent ligands in the top 1%; the study achieves one.

What the negative results buy. Three findings in this report would have been invisible in a conventional write-up. The optimistic cross-validation AUC was exactly 1.000 and the honest one was 0.961, which quantifies the cost of feature-selection leakage in a small dataset. The same models produced a large apparent enrichment of MI-indication molecules against the training decoys and no enrichment at all against other approved drugs (p ≈ 0.59), which shows that decoy-based enrichment is not evidence of pharmacological validity; the legacy decoy analysis and the reasons it is not independent validation are documented in Appendix B rather than used as a result here. And the top-ranked drug and the second-ranked drug share a scaffold, one of which is not a PDE4 inhibitor, which shows that chemotype similarity can masquerade as mechanism. Each of these is a concrete, reusable caution for ligand-based repurposing, and each is only visible because the controls were run.

Assessment against the objectives. Objective 1 (a reproducible pipeline) is met: every stage writes a versioned table and a log, and the whole analysis can be re-run from raw GEO files. Objective 2 (quantifying optimism) is met for feature selection, threshold selection and scaffold splitting, and the three estimates are reported side by side. Objective 3 (concordant targets) is met for PDE4B only; EPAS1 and PFKFB3 remain hypotheses with unresolved directionality. Objective 4 (adversarial evaluation before application) is met, and it is the reason the shortlist is presented with qualifications rather than as a deliverable. Objective 5 (an uncertainty-aware shortlist) is partially met: the first position is stable under every aggregation rule tested, positions 2–10 are aggregation-dependent, and positions beyond 10 are not reported as candidates.

Answer to the research questions. RQ1: the signature discriminates within the discovery cell fraction but does not transfer across cell fractions; transportability is not supported. RQ2: one gene, PDE4B, passes the strict rule; six more pass only a relaxed rule introduced after inspection, and their evidence is exploratory. RQ3: ligand-based ranking carries target-specific information but does not reliably recover known actives; its apparent performance is inflated by benchmark easiness. RQ4: approved AMI drugs are not enriched, so the ranking does not capture indication-level pharmacology.

Final statement. The deliverable of this project is therefore not a list of drugs to test. It is a prioritised target (PDE4B, with a testable mechanistic hypothesis and an independent experimental literature), an exploratory pipeline whose biases are measured rather than assumed away, and a documented account of the ways in which the current ranking misleads – decoy-based enrichment, chemotype similarity, molecular size, and non-transportable thresholds. In a field where near-perfect benchmark numbers are cheap, the ability to say precisely which numbers are real is the more transferable result.

Two further limitations of the audit itself should be stated. It is an internal audit: the same analyst who built the pipeline also searched for its errors, so there may be issues that remain undetected. The decision rules in Section 3.3 were fixed before the corresponding results were inspected, but the pipeline as a whole evolved. The repository history, with one commit per stage, preserves that evolution and allows any claim in this report to be traced to the script and table that produced it.
