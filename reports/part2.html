4. Methods and Materials

The pipeline has five stages: data assembly, transcriptomic modelling, target prioritisation, ligand modelling and approved-drug ranking. Each stage writes a versioned table and a human-readable log; no number in this report exists only inside a figure. Figure 1 shows the overall workflow, and Table 1 summarises the three cohorts.

Figure 1. Overview of the computational workflow as actually executed. Four blocks are separated by vertical rules: the transcriptomic branch (download, parsing and QC, limma, stability selection, nested cross-validation), target annotation (Open Targets, STRING, ChEMBL, rule-based screen), the ligand benchmark (actives and decoys, ECFP4 features, scaffold-grouped splits, model comparison with hard negatives) and the independent drug evaluation (approved-drug pool, three-target scoring and consensus ranking, exploratory MI-indication test, shortlist). No graph neural network, DeepPurpose model, docking or free-energy calculation was run. All intermediate tables are retained in results/tables/ and all logs in logs/.

4.1 Data sources and cohort definitions

Two public Gene Expression Omnibus series were used, both on the Affymetrix HG-U133 Plus 2.0 platform (GPL570), which removes platform effects as a confounder and leaves cell composition as the dominant difference between them.

GSE66360 contains 99 samples of CD146-positive circulating endothelial cells, isolated from 49 patients with AMI and 50 healthy controls [1]. The submitters recorded a discovery/validation split in the sample titles (43 discovery samples, 56 validation samples). We used the 43 discovery samples as the training set (21 cases, 22 controls) and the 56 validation samples as an internal, same-cohort validation set (28 cases, 28 controls). The split is therefore external to our analysis and could not be influenced by it.

GSE48060 contains 52 peripheral blood samples: 21 normal controls and 31 patients with first-time AMI sampled within 48 hours, of whom 5 had recurrent events during 18 months of follow-up and 26 did not [2]. We defined cases as all 31 first-time AMI patients and controls as the 21 normal controls. This choice discards the original prognostic contrast (recurrent vs non-recurrent) in favour of a diagnostic contrast, which is the question of this study; the consequence – that 5 cases are clinically distinct from the other 26 – is carried into the limitations. The cohort was used exclusively for external evaluation and never for feature selection, threshold selection or model selection.

4.2 Preprocessing

Each series matrix was parsed without transformation, and probe-level intensities were mapped to gene symbols using the GPL570 annotation file. Where several probes mapped to the same gene, the probe with the highest mean intensity across samples was retained; this rule replaced an earlier plan to take the median, and the change was recorded in the log because it can alter the differential-expression result (Section 5.12). Probe selection was performed once on all 99 GSE66360 samples (including the 56 samples later used as the internal validation subset), while the external cohort selected its probes independently from its own 52 samples. This step is therefore upstream of the cross-validation split and is not covered by the fold-wise re-estimation described in Section 4.4. Probes without a gene symbol were discarded. Both datasets yielded 21,755 genes; 54,675 probes were reduced to 21,755 genes (23,363 probes collapsed, 60.2% of probes lost), identically in both cohorts because the mapping depends only on the platform annotation.

Both matrices were already log2-scaled (maximum 14.9 and 15.4 respectively), so no further transformation was applied. No cross-dataset normalisation, batch correction or ComBat step was performed at this stage: applying a batch-correction method estimated on the external cohort would leak information across the train/test boundary, and applying one estimated on the training cohort alone would leave the external data unadjusted anyway. A within-dataset z-score sensitivity analysis was run post hoc and is reported in Section 5.2.

4.3 Differential expression

Differential expression was estimated with limma 3.68.4 using a group-means design and the contrast case − control, with empirical Bayes moderation and Benjamini–Hochberg control of the false discovery rate [18]. The analysis was run on the 43 training samples only. The primary threshold was |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05; a relaxed threshold (|log2FC| > 0.5) was recorded for descriptive purposes but not used for feature selection. The same procedure was applied separately to the 56 internal validation samples, purely to estimate replication; those results never entered feature selection.

Critically, the same limma procedure was re-run inside every outer training fold of the nested cross-validation, using a dedicated R helper script invoked from Python. The gene pool of a fold therefore depends only on that fold's training samples.

4.4 Signature selection and three-tier validation

Signature selection used stability selection over 200 stratified bootstrap resamples of the training set. In each resample a random forest (500 trees) ranked genes by impurity importance and an L1-penalised logistic regression (C = 1.0, liblinear) selected a sparse set; the intersection of the forest's top 20 with the L1 non-zero set defined a “hit”, and the selection frequency of a gene was the proportion of resamples in which it was hit. Genes with frequency ≥ 0.50 were retained; if fewer than five genes qualified, the top ten ranked by frequency and then by mean importance were taken and the fallback was recorded. Only one gene reached 0.50 (PDE4B, 0.785), so the fallback was triggered; this is reported as a stability result, not as a technical detail.

Three performance estimates were produced from the same 43 samples, and their ordering is the point of the design:

  • Optimistic (train_cv_optimistic): features selected on all 43 samples, then five-fold cross-validation on the same samples. This is the estimate that most student projects report.
  • Fixed pool (train_nested_fixedpool): the gene pool is the 398 differentially expressed genes of the full training set, but stability selection and model fitting are re-done inside each fold. This removes selection leakage at the level of the final signature but not pre-filtering bias at the level of the gene pool.
  • Fully nested (train_nested_full, primary): limma, stability selection and model fitting are all re-estimated inside each outer training fold. Nothing from the held-out fold – not even the list of differentially expressed genes – influences the model.

The final model was a logistic regression with L2 penalty (C = 1.0) on the ten signature genes. The classifier was chosen by comparing L2 logistic regression with a random forest on the discovery folds; this choice was made outside the outer evaluation loop, so the nested estimates do not include model-selection uncertainty. Prediction on the internal and external cohorts used the model fitted on all 43 training samples, with the decision threshold fixed at 0.6001 and, as a secondary reference, 0.5. The 0.6001 threshold originates from an early out-of-fold analysis of the discovery cohort in which features had been selected on all samples; it was carried forward unchanged and was not re-optimised inside the final nested procedure, nor on either validation cohort. Missing genes in the external cohort were imputed with training-set medians; none were missing.

4.5 Target prioritisation

Target prioritisation started not from the ten-gene signature but from the union of the five fold-specific signatures (28 distinct genes), because a gene that enters four of five fold signatures but misses the final ten is not thereby irrelevant. Each gene was annotated with its selection frequency and with a flag indicating membership of the final signature. Three public resources were queried through their REST APIs, with three retries and exponential back-off, and every failure was logged rather than imputed:

  • Open Targets Platform (GraphQL v4): overall association score for myocardial infarction (MONDO_0005068, resolved by search rather than hard-coded), the datatype decomposition, tractability annotations, and the number of known drugs from drugAndClinicalCandidates.
  • STRING: number of interaction partners and maximum combined score.
  • ChEMBL: number of distinct molecules with measured activity and the maximum pChEMBL value.

Two rules were applied. The strict rule required an Open Targets overall score ≥ 0.10. The relaxed rule required ≥ 0.05 or at least one known drug. Fourteen genes could not be mapped to ChEMBL targets and were recorded with a missing value; they were not removed from the annotation table.

4.6 Ligand dataset construction

Four targets had sufficient ChEMBL chemistry to support a ligand model: PDE4B, PFKFB3, EPAS1 and PDK4. CXCL16, METRNL and HBEGF had no qualifying activity data and were therefore annotated but not modelled; this is a data-availability constraint that biases the modelled set towards well-studied targets and is discussed in Section 5.12.

Actives were extracted from ChEMBL with pChEMBL ≥ 6, standardised with RDKit (largest fragment, charge neutralisation, tautomer canonicalisation), filtered on molecular weight ≤ 800, and de-duplicated on the first 14 characters of the InChIKey. PDE4B had 1,651 unique molecules, more than the modelling budget allowed, so the 800 with the highest pChEMBL were retained. This cap is consequential and is revisited in Section 5.8: it restricts the positive class to ligands with pChEMBL ≥ 7.58 (IC50 ≤ 26 nM), which is one to two orders of magnitude more potent than most approved drugs.

Decoys were drawn from a pool of 27,930 ChEMBL small molecules and matched to each active on molecular weight (±50 Da), cLogP (±1), hydrogen-bond donors (±1), acceptors (±1), rotatable bonds (±2) and ring count (±1), with an explicit Tanimoto ceiling of 0.4 to the active. Ten decoys per active were requested; 7.8–10.0 were obtained, and the shortfall was recorded rather than padded. The final pools contained 1,361 actives and 11,773 decoys.

4.7 Scaffold-aware splits and models

Splits were generated once from Bemis–Murcko scaffolds [8] and frozen to disk; every subsequent experiment reused the same file. Grouping was on the canonical scaffold SMILES (InChIKey14 for scaffold-free molecules), with stratified group five-fold assignment. Molecules were represented by 2,048-bit ECFP4 fingerprints [6]; nine physicochemical descriptors were used as a comparator feature set, standardised within each training fold. Three classifiers were compared: L2 logistic regression (C = 1.0, balanced), random forest (500 trees, balanced subsampling) and XGBoost (300 trees, depth 6, positive-class weighting). Random forest was pre-registered as the main model if the mean PR-AUC gap to XGBoost across the three main targets was below 0.02; the observed gap was 0.0085, so random forest was selected and never changed.

PDK4 had only 44 actives and was analysed with a single 80/20 scaffold hold-out and flagged exploratory throughout.

4.8 Metrics

For each benchmark we report ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, enrichment factor at 1% and 5% (EF1, EF5), precision at 10 (p@10), the positive prevalence, and the lift (PR-AUC divided by prevalence). The prevalence and lift are essential: PR-AUC is not comparable across benchmarks with different positive rates, whereas ROC-AUC and lift are. Because the top 1% of a pool of several thousand molecules contains more molecules than the pool contains actives, EF1 is bounded above by 1/prevalence; a value at that bound means the metric is saturated rather than excellent, and we say so explicitly in Section 5.5.

4.9 Diagnostic experiments

Four diagnostics were run before any approved drug was scored.

(i) Leakage audit. Overlap between actives and decoys before and after standardisation, and the number of scaffold or InChIKey groups spanning folds, were computed for every target.

(ii) Label permutation. Within each training fold the labels were shuffled and a random forest was trained on the shuffled labels and used to predict the untouched test fold. Under this null, PR-AUC should fall to the prevalence and ROC-AUC to 0.5. This tests for pipeline-level leakage rather than for biological signal.

(iii) Cross-target hard negatives. Each target's model was asked to score the actives of a different target. A target-specific model should place other targets' ligands below its own; a model that has merely learned “drug-like” should place them high. The fraction predicted positive and the median percentile against the model's own ligand distribution were recorded.

(iv) Prevalence-matched down-sampling. The PDE4B hard-negative benchmark has a positive prevalence of 43.6%, four times that of the easy benchmark, so its PR-AUC cannot be compared directly. The easy benchmark was therefore down-sampled to the same prevalence using the stored out-of-fold predictions, repeated 20 times.

4.10 Approved-drug scoring and shortlist construction

The scoring library comprised all ChEMBL small molecules with maximum clinical phase 4 (3,475 records). After standardisation (3,083), InChIKey14 de-duplication (1,988), removal of molecules present in any training set (3) and removal of molecules in the AMI indication set (80), 1,905 molecules remained. The three main-target models were refitted on their full training pools – an acceptable procedure because the library is disjoint from all training data of the three main-target models – and each molecule received three scores. Two aggregations were computed: the arithmetic mean of the three scores, and the minimum rank across targets. A third, rank-based aggregation (mean percentile) was added post hoc in Section 5.10.

The AMI indication set was obtained from ChEMBL indications containing “myocardial infarction” (161 molecules, 108 with usable structures, 89 after InChIKey14 de-duplication). These 89 molecules were scored with the same models but were excluded from the shortlist, so that the enrichment test in Section 4.11 used molecules the shortlist could not contain.

4.11 Indication enrichment test

For each target, the MI-indication molecules were compared with the remaining approved drugs within that target's own evaluation set, after excluding every molecule that overlaps the corresponding model's training data (positives and decoys): PDE4B 86 vs 1,844; PFKFB3 89 vs 1,888; EPAS1 88 vs 1,879; consensus 86 vs 1,823. The test was one-sided Mann–Whitney (alternative: MI-indication molecules score higher), with an AUC-like statistic equal to U/(n1n2) with ties counted as half, and top-1%/top-5% enrichment factors. A non-significant result was to be reported as “no enrichment observed” without further subgroup searching.

4.12 Software, reproducibility and deviations from plan

Python 3.13 with numpy 2.3 [22], pandas 2.3 [23], scikit-learn 1.7 [19], RDKit 2026.09 [21], XGBoost 3.4 [20], SciPy 1.16 and matplotlib 3.10; R 4.6.1 with limma 3.68.4 [18] and the GPL570 annotation package. All random seeds were fixed at 42, splits were written to disk before modelling, and every script is version-controlled with one commit per pipeline stage. The full code and intermediate tables are available at the repository linked on the title page.

Five deviations from the original plan are recorded. (1) The target rule was relaxed after the strict rule returned one target; the relaxed analysis is exploratory. (2) The probe-collapsing rule changed from median to maximum-mean after preprocessing showed that the median rule discarded high-intensity probes; no sensitivity analysis was run. (3) The PDE4B positive set was capped at 800 molecules, a decision taken for computational reasons before modelling. (4) The aggregation of target scores was initially the arithmetic mean; the rank-based aggregation was added after inspection. (5) The 108-to-89 de-duplication of AMI drugs changed the denominator of the enrichment test after the first pass; the final test uses 89 throughout.
