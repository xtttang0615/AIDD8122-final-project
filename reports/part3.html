5. Results and Discussion

5.1 Cohorts, preprocessing and differential expression

Both cohorts passed the scale check (maxima 14.9 and 15.4, i.e. already log2) and mapped to the same 21,755 genes. Differential expression in the 43 training samples identified 398 genes at |log2FC| > 1 and 5% FDR, of which 370 were up-regulated and only 28 down-regulated (Figure 2). The strongly asymmetric direction is biologically coherent: what changes in circulating endothelial cells after AMI is predominantly the induction of an activation programme – adhesion, chemokine and immediate-early transcription factor genes – rather than the loss of constitutive endothelial identity. The five most significant genes were PDE4B (log2FC = 1.19, FDR = 4.4 × 10−7), CXCL16 (2.44), NFIL3 (2.20), ZFP36 (1.56) and SKIL (2.60); among the strongest fold changes were ITLN1 (3.61), PDK4 (3.45) and EPAS1 (2.32).

Figure 2. Volcano plot of the 21,755 genes in the training set (n = 43; 21 cases, 22 controls). Thresholds: |log2FC| > 1 and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05 (398 genes; 370 up, 28 down). PDE4B, the only gene selected in all five cross-validation folds, is among the most significant up-regulated genes.

Replication in the internal validation subset was partial but directionally perfect: 190 genes were significant at the same threshold in both subsets, and the sign of the log fold change agreed for every one of the 190. The overlap rate is defined as the intersection divided by the mean size of the two significant sets, i.e. 190 / mean(398, 600) = 38.1%, not 190/398. This is a stronger result than the raw overlap suggests, because the two subsets are different patients from the same study and the same cell fraction; it establishes technical reproducibility, not biological generalisation. It would be a mistake to read the 38% overlap as evidence that the signature generalises to other cohorts – the external cohort addresses that question, and answers it differently (Section 5.2).

5.2 Signature performance across three tiers

Applying the three nested designs to the same 43 samples produced three clearly separated estimates (Table 2, Figure 3). The optimistic estimate, in which features were selected on all samples before cross-validation, was AUC 1.000. Moving the gene pool outside the folds (fixed pool) reduced this to 0.950. Re-estimating differential expression inside each fold as well (fully nested) gave 0.961 (95% CI 0.890–1.000), with sensitivity 0.905 and specificity 0.955 at the locked threshold. The difference between the optimistic and nested estimates (0.039 AUC) is the price of honest feature selection in a 43-sample dataset, and it is the single most reproducible methodological finding of this study: the perfect score was an artefact, and it was an artefact of exactly the kind that dominates this literature.

Two results in Table 2 deserve emphasis. First, the external AUC of 0.608 has a confidence interval that comfortably includes 0.5: by the pre-specified criterion R6 (AUC ≥ 0.75), transfer is not supported. Second, at the locked threshold the external sensitivity is exactly zero while specificity is 1.000. This is not a modelling bug; it is a statement about probability distributions. The mean predicted probability in the external cohort was 0.0079, against 0.3454 in the internal cohort, so essentially every external sample fell below a threshold calibrated on a different distribution. A zero-sensitivity result of this kind is sometimes reported as “the signature fails”, but the more precise statement is that the decision rule fails while the ranking retains weak information (AUC 0.608).

The post-hoc z-score analysis makes this distinction concrete and is worth reporting in full because it is counter-intuitive. Standardising each dataset internally raised sensitivity from 0.00 to 0.58 and specificity to 0.62 – the threshold now “works” – but the AUC fell from 0.608 to 0.575, the calibration slope collapsed to 0.089, and the Brier score (0.315) became worse than the trivial baseline of always predicting 0.5 (0.25). In other words, re-standardisation restored the marginal distribution without restoring the signal; the classifier became usable only in the sense that it began to predict both classes at random-ish rates. Reporting sensitivity alone would have converted a failure into an apparent success, which is precisely why the AUC and the calibration slope are reported alongside it (Figure 4).

5.3 High discrimination, low stability

The signature itself is much less stable than its AUC suggests. Of the 398 candidate genes, only PDE4B reached a bootstrap selection frequency above 0.50 (0.785); the second-ranked gene, GADD45A, reached 0.430 (Table 3). Because fewer than five genes met the stability criterion, the pre-specified fallback selected the top ten by frequency and importance. The ten genes are PDE4B, GADD45A, CXCL16, ZFP36, SKIL, SYTL3, SAMSN1, EPAS1, ITLN1 and RGS1, all up-regulated, and all biologically plausible in an activated endothelial context (chemokine signalling, immediate-early transcription, TGF-β signalling, hypoxia response). But plausibility is not stability.

The fold-level behaviour is the more informative statistic. The five fold-specific signatures involved 28 distinct genes in total, and any two folds shared only 3–5 genes (Jaccard 0.176–0.333). PDE4B was the only gene present in all five. A model built on ten genes of which one is reproducible is, in effect, a model built on one gene plus nine correlates of it. This does not invalidate the AUC – the model genuinely discriminates in this cohort – but it does mean that the signature should not be presented as a generalisable ten-gene panel. We therefore describe it throughout as a “ten-gene signature” in the descriptive sense only.

Three observations follow. First, the fact that the strict rule returns a single gene is itself a result: among 28 candidates, only PDE4B has both a substantial disease association and existing chemical matter. Second, the relaxed rule was introduced after seeing this, and everything downstream that depends on the seven-gene list is therefore exploratory; we flag this rather than presenting the seven as equally supported. Third, the evidence for the seven is not equal: PFKFB3 appears in only one of five folds and is absent from the final signature, yet it was modelled with the same weight as PDE4B. That decision was driven by data availability – PFKFB3 has 273 ChEMBL actives – not by the strength of the transcriptomic evidence, and it is a genuine inconsistency in the design.

Two features of this table matter more than the headline numbers. First, the benchmark is saturated. Second, fingerprints carry the signal, but not all of it is substructural. Replacing ECFP4 with nine physicochemical descriptors dropped PR-AUC from 0.993 to 0.226 for PDE4B, from 0.968 to 0.296 for PFKFB3 and from 0.989 to 0.259 for EPAS1, while ROC-AUC remained between 0.64 and 0.86 (0.642, 0.793 and 0.858; Table 5 and Figure 6). The pattern is diagnostic: a substantial part of the class separation is achievable from size, lipophilicity and hydrogen-bonding alone (descriptor-only ROC-AUC 0.64–0.86 across the three targets), and the fingerprints add the substructure resolution needed to reach high precision. This is exactly the regime in which a model applied to an external library will be dominated by “does it look like the training chemotypes” rather than “does it bind the target”, and it anticipates the control analysis in Section 5.8.

5.6 Diagnostics: is there leakage, and is the signal target-specific?

Leakage. The audit found no overlap between actives and decoys before or after standardisation (0 in all four targets) and no scaffold or InChIKey group spanning folds (0 cross-fold keys). Label permutation produced ROC-AUC of 0.499, 0.491 and 0.386 and PR-AUC of 0.130, 0.094 and 0.069 – that is, at or below the positive prevalence (0.114, 0.093, 0.091). A pipeline that leaked information from test to train would show permutation PR-AUC well above prevalence; this one does not. The high PR-AUC is therefore not a plumbing error.

Target specificity. Cross-target hard negatives gave a more nuanced answer. When the PDE4B model was asked to score PFKFB3 actives, 17.6% exceeded its decision threshold and the median PFKFB3 ligand sat at the 88.5th percentile of the PDE4B score distribution; for EPAS1 actives the figures were 16.0% and 88.6. In the reverse direction, the PFKFB3 model placed only 4.1% of PDE4B actives above threshold, but their median percentile was still 90.0. The asymmetry is informative: the PDE4B model is permissive towards other targets' ligands, while the PFKFB3 model is not. Two conclusions follow. First, the models do capture target-specific information – other targets' ligands are not scored as highly as the target's own. Second, they also capture a large general component that lifts any drug-like ligand far above random decoys, which is why the median percentile of a foreign ligand is near 90 rather than near 50.

5.7 Approved-drug ranking and the shortlist

The scoring library contained 1,905 approved small molecules after removal of training-set positives (3) and AMI-indication drugs (80). All 1,905 received all three scores. A further 82 molecules overlap a training-negative (decoy) set of at least one of the three main-target models, so the final evaluation library used from here on contains 1,823 molecules. The score distributions are extremely skewed: the median PDE4B model score was 0.020, the 99th percentile 0.215 (linear interpolation), and the maximum 0.890; only two molecules exceeded 0.5 (Figure 8A; values in library_score_stats.csv). This skew is itself a finding: the models were trained on nanomolar ligands, and most approved drugs simply do not resemble them.

Ranking by the arithmetic mean of the three scores produced a shortlist dominated by large, multi-ring kinase inhibitors (Figure 7, Table 7). This is an aggregation artefact with a traceable cause. The three models are not on a common probability scale: the maximum PDE4B score is 0.890 whereas the maxima for PFKFB3 and EPAS1 are 0.188 and 0.276, and the medians differ by a factor of two. Averaging three differently calibrated probabilities rewards molecules that receive a middling score from all three models over molecules that receive an outstanding score from one. In the limit, the mean ranking converges on “the most generically drug-like molecule in the library”. Vericiguat and finerenone illustrate the problem: both are cardiovascular drugs that appear at ranks 8 and 9 of the PDE4B list (scores 0.290 and 0.272), yet neither has any mechanistic relationship to PDE4 – vericiguat is a soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator and finerenone a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist. Their high PDE4B scores reflect chemotype, not pharmacology.

That coherence is encouraging, but it is also a warning, because one of the two has no confirmed PDE4 activity (Section 5.8).

5.8 Control analysis: one hit, one miss, one unconfirmed high scorer

The value of a ranking can only be judged against molecules with known pharmacology. Three approved drugs in the library have unambiguous, independently documented PDE4 status.

  • Crisaborole (CHEMBL484785), a benzoxaborole PDE4 inhibitor approved topically for atopic dermatitis, scored 0.890 for PDE4B and ranked 1st of 1,823. It was not used in training: its InChIKey14 (USZAGAREISWJDP) is absent from the PDE4B active set. This is a genuine out-of-training hit and the strongest single piece of evidence in the study.
  • Ibudilast (CHEMBL19449), a PDE4 inhibitor approved in Japan, with a reported IC50 of 65 nM against human PDE4B [15], scored 0.008 and ranked 1,282nd of 1,823 – below the median of the library. It was also absent from training. This is a clear false negative.
  • Tavaborole (CHEMBL443052), a benzoxaborole approved for onychomycosis whose confirmed target is leucyl-tRNA synthetase, scored 0.508 and ranked 2nd. No measured PDE4B activity is available for this compound in the sources used here, so it is reported as a high-scoring candidate whose PDE4B activity is unconfirmed; its position next to the true positive is most parsimoniously explained by the shared benzoxaborole chemotype.

By the pre-specified criterion R6 – at least two independent known ligands recovered in the top 1% – the ligand model fails. Its sensitivity among evaluable known PDE4 inhibitors is 1/2, and its top-ranked pair contains one true positive and one chemically related neighbour whose PDE4B activity is unconfirmed. This is a materially different conclusion from the one an earlier version of this analysis reached, which counted crisaborole as the sole positive control and described the result as successful. The correction matters for two reasons.

First, the reason crisaborole was outside the training set is not that it is a novel molecule; it is that the PDE4B positive set was truncated at the 800 most potent ligands, and crisaborole (pChEMBL 7.26) fell just below the cut-off of 7.58. It is therefore a potency-truncated hold-out rather than an independent test molecule: its ChEMBL record exists, its analogues are in the training set, and the only reason the model had not seen it is that the positive class was restricted to IC50 ≤ 26 nM. Calling this “external validation” overstates what was done.

Second, the ibudilast miss has a plausible mechanism that also explains the whole shortlist. The model was trained exclusively on nanomolar ligands and applied to a library of approved drugs whose target potencies are typically micromolar. Ibudilast, at 65 nM, is potent but structurally unrelated to the PDE4B training chemotypes (it is a pyrazolopyridine, not a catechol ether or benzoxaborole), so it is penalised for chemotype rather than rewarded for potency. Crisaborole, at 0.49 µM, is weaker but structurally close to training analogues, so it is rewarded. The ranking therefore encodes resemblance to potent training chemotypes, which is a different quantity from probability of binding, and the two can diverge sharply – as they do here, in opposite directions.

The molecular-size analysis in Figure 8B makes the same point quantitatively. Across the 1,823-molecule evaluation library, the PDE4B model score correlates with molecular weight (Spearman ρ = 0.396) and, weakly, with cLogP (ρ = 0.093); the corresponding consensus-score correlations are 0.458 and 0.033. These are associations only: the correlation with size does not by itself establish a causal mechanism, and it does not show that any particular molecule was misranked. The top 20 molecules by PDE4B score have a mean molecular weight of 487 Da against 360 Da for the 1,823-molecule library as a whole, and the PDE4B training actives have a median of 481 Da. The two benzoxaboroles are the exceptions that prove the rule: they are small (251 and 152 Da) and yet score highly, which is precisely what a genuine substructure-driven signal should look like against a background of size-driven scoring. The ranking thus contains two distinguishable components – a specific benzoxaborole signal and a general bulk/hydrogen-bonding signal – and only the first is pharmacologically meaningful.

5.9 Indication enrichment: a pre-specified negative result

The orthogonal test was whether ChEMBL molecules with a registered myocardial-infarction indication score higher than other approved drugs, after excluding molecules that overlap each model's training data. They do not. For PDE4B the median score was 0.018 in MI-indication molecules versus 0.020 in the remainder (one-sided p = 0.589, AUC-like 0.493); for PFKFB3, 0.010 versus 0.012 (p = 0.736, 0.480); for EPAS1, 0.010 versus 0.010 (p = 0.483, 0.501); for the consensus score, 0.017 versus 0.018 (p = 0.750, 0.479). Every AUC-like value is within 0.02 of 0.5 and every p-value is far from significance (Figure 9). Each target is tested against its own evaluation set after removing that model's training overlaps, so the four denominators differ (1,930, 1,977, 1,967 and 1,909 molecules); the consensus row is the only one computed on the 1,823-molecule library used in Sections 5.8–5.10.

Figure 9. Per-target sets differ: PDE4B 86 vs 1,844, PFKFB3 89 vs 1,888, EPAS1 88 vs 1,879, consensus 86 vs 1,823. This negative result is the most instructive comparison in the study, because the same models produced an apparently spectacular enrichment only one step earlier. Against random decoys, the 89 de-duplicated MI-indication molecules scored far above the decoy median (PDE4B: top-1% enrichment 19.6×, Mann–Whitney p = 1.4 × 10−34). These legacy decoy numbers must not be read as independent validation. Three problems apply: (i) the decoy background is the same pool the models were trained against, so the comparison is not independent; (ii) an earlier version of the scoring script mapped molecule identities to scores by truncating the input table after featurisation failures, so per-molecule assignments in the archived table were wrong for part of the set (the aggregate distribution was unaffected because the multiset of scores was preserved, but individual rows were not reliable); (iii) molecules overlapping the training data were not excluded at that stage. Against other approved drugs, after correcting all three issues, the same molecules show nothing. The two results are not in conflict; they measure different things. The decoy comparison asks “are these molecules drug-like?” and the answer is trivially yes. The approved-drug comparison asks “are these molecules more likely to act on this target?” and the answer is no.

This is worth stating plainly because decoy-based enrichment is routinely reported as evidence of model validity in the repurposing literature. Our data show that a model can produce a 20-fold decoy enrichment with p ≈ 10−34 and still be unable to distinguish cardiovascular drugs from antivirals. Decoy enrichment is a necessary but very weak condition; without an indication-level or target-level control, it should not be interpreted as validation.

5.10 Post-hoc robustness of the shortlist

Because the arithmetic mean of probabilities is sensitive to scale, we recomputed the ranking on the same final candidate set (n = 1,823; MI-indication molecules and any molecule overlapping the training data of one of the three main-target models excluded; the exploratory PDK4 model is not used for exclusion) using the mean percentile of the three per-target scores, a rule that is invariant to each model's calibration scale. The two orderings agree closely overall (Spearman ρ = 0.952) but not at the top: only 11 of the 20 top molecules are shared, and 30 of 50 in the top 50. A Borda rank-sum rule gives a near-identical ordering to the mean percentile (Spearman ρ = 0.9999; 20/20 top-20 shared), so it is reported only as a supplementary check and is not independent evidence. In other words, the identity of individual candidates below the very top is not robust to a defensible change in aggregation rule. Crisaborole ranks first under all three rules (PDE4B score 0.890, PDE4B rank 1/1,823), whereas tavaborole is 2nd by PDE4B score but only 49th by mean percentile, because its PFKFB3 and EPAS1 scores are low – a concrete illustration that single-target and consensus rankings are not interchangeable.

5.11 What the results do and do not support

Supported. (i) A ten-gene CEC signature discriminates AMI from healthy controls within the discovery cohort (nested AUC 0.961), and the discrimination is not an artefact of feature-selection leakage, because the leakage-controlled estimate is explicitly reported alongside the optimistic one. (ii) The signature does not transfer to whole blood (AUC 0.608, CI including 0.5), and the locked threshold produces zero sensitivity; transfer across cell fractions is not supported. (iii) PDE4B is the only gene supported simultaneously by signature stability (5/5 folds), disease annotation (0.266), chemical tractability (28 known drugs) and independent experimental literature showing that PDE4B deletion or inhibition is cardioprotective in ischaemia–reperfusion [12]. Its direction of change in patients (up-regulated) is concordant with the direction of the therapeutic hypothesis (inhibit). (iv) The ligand models contain target-specific information (hard-negative lifts of 2.3–9.7 over chance) but were trained and evaluated on saturated benchmarks. (v) The approved-drug ranking does not enrich for AMI indication.

Not supported. (i) That the shortlist identifies effective repurposing candidates. With one true positive, one false negative and one high-scoring neighbour with unconfirmed activity among three evaluable controls, and no indication-level enrichment, the ranking has not demonstrated prospective value. (ii) That crisaborole constitutes external validation. It is a potency-truncated hold-out from the same ChEMBL target record, and it is chemically close to the training set. (iii) That EPAS1 and PFKFB3 are druggable in the required direction. Both are supported as targets but the desired pharmacology is activation (HIF-2α) or preservation (PFKFB3) of angiogenesis, whereas the available chemical matter and therefore the model's bias point towards inhibition [16,17]. Any inhibitor nominated for these targets by this pipeline is not supported by the current evidence, because the model's chemical bias points away from the direction the biology would require. (iv) That the arithmetic-mean ranking is a consensus. It is an aggregation of three differently calibrated scores, and it is not robust to a change of aggregation rule.

5.12 Limitations

Sample size and stability. The training set contains 43 samples and 398 candidate genes. The nested AUC has a confidence interval spanning 0.890–1.000, and only one gene survives stability selection. Any ten-gene panel derived from this cohort should be regarded as provisional until replicated in an independent CEC cohort.

Cell fraction and external validity. The discovery data are CD146+ CECs and the external data are whole blood. The AUC drop from 0.842 to 0.608 is therefore confounded with a change in cell composition, and we cannot separate “the signature does not generalise” from “the signature is measured in the wrong cells”. This is the single largest limitation of the transcriptomic layer.

Case definition in the external cohort. Five of the 31 external cases had recurrent events during follow-up and may differ clinically from the other 26. Merging them is defensible for a diagnostic contrast but adds heterogeneity that a larger cohort would allow us to model explicitly.

Preprocessing choices. The probe-collapsing rule (maximum-mean rather than median) was changed mid-project and no sensitivity analysis was run. A different rule would change the gene list, and possibly the DEG count, although the direction of the major genes is unlikely to reverse.

Potency-truncated positive class. The PDE4B positives are restricted to pChEMBL ≥ 7.58. This makes the training distribution unrepresentative of approved drugs, which typically bind with micromolar potency, and it produces exactly the false negative observed for ibudilast. A potency-stratified training set, or a regression on pChEMBL rather than a binary classifier, would be the appropriate remedy.

Decoy bias. Despite property matching and a Tanimoto ceiling, the decoys remain trivially separable; the near-perfect PR-AUC should not be read as prospective screening power. The indication-level test in Section 5.9 quantifies part of this gap.

Direction-agnostic scoring. The models score ligands, not effects. For PDE4B this happens to align with the therapeutic hypothesis; for EPAS1 and PFKFB3 it does not. A pipeline that cannot represent agonism versus antagonism should not nominate modulators of targets whose desired direction is uncertain.

Target selection by data availability. The four modelled targets were those with sufficient ChEMBL chemistry. CXCL16, METRNL and HBEGF were annotated but not modelled despite reasonable transcriptomic evidence, which biases the target set towards well-studied proteins.

Analytical flexibility. Two rules were added after inspection (relaxed target rule, rank-based aggregation). Both are labelled, but their existence means that the exploratory parts of the analysis are not confirmatory.

No experimental validation. No wet-lab experiment was performed. All pharmacological statements about the shortlist are inferences from public data.

5.13 Future work

Four directions follow directly from the limitations. First, the positive class should be rebuilt as a potency-stratified or regression target, so that approved drugs at micromolar potency are inside the training distribution rather than outside it. Second, a proper prospective benchmark is needed: a set of PDE4 inhibitors held out by molecule rather than by potency truncation, ideally including piclamilast, cilomilast and additional benzoxaboroles, would allow sensitivity and specificity to be estimated with more than three control molecules. Third, the direction problem can be addressed with pharmacology-aware labels (agonist/antagonist/activator) or, where structures exist, with docking or free-energy calculations that at least distinguish binding modes. Fourth, the transcriptomic layer should be tested in an independent CEC cohort before the ten-gene panel is described as a signature; if none is available, targeted qPCR of PDE4B, CXCL16 and EPAS1 in a prospective AMI series would be a cheap and decisive experiment. On the wet-lab side, the natural next step is to test whether a PDE4B-selective compound reduces neutrophil adhesion to TNF-α-stimulated endothelial cells, which is the specific mechanism proposed by Wan et al. [12] and the one most directly connected to the signature reported here.
